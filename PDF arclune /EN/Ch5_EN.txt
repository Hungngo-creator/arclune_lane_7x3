Chapter 5 — Lettercraft & a Rival

Morning. I folded the cot in the storeroom, slid the wooden latch; cold ink still on my hands. Stepped into the alley. Wet-wood smell lived here. Overhead, an aether freight car rolled across the steel bridge; the tin roof of the porridge stall shivered a beat, then stilled. The Outer stayed the same: trades under the bridge, cargo above. The alley breathed slow. The bell at the mouth of the lane tapped once, then went quiet. Up high, the inner face of the Middle Ring cast down a thin halo—some 3D ad for the Name Inscribing Festival drifted over the corrugated roofs, letters floating like mist. From the Outer you only saw a ghost of it—enough to know the other side was buzzing. I pulled out a chair, set the brush kit down, chose a small board, ground ink, tested stroke. Tham Lang flicked a fingernail against the teapot: “We’ve got one watch of good hour left.”

“Enough,” I said. Didn’t need more. I ruled the margin, breathed steady—Breathwork ticked up half a tenth; lungs cool as if rinsed with silver.

Numbers in my head gathered—plus eight. 719.

“How do we price rush locks?”
“Don’t shout a price,” I said, glancing back. A locksmith had tucked himself against the wall, watching me repair SỬA NHANH—QUICK FIX—into SỬA KHÉO—FIX WITH CARE. I added a thin line, spidery letters: rush jobs don’t get price-gouged.

——

My stomach remembered me. Across the lane, the porridge lady—round as I’d left her—smiled with her eyes the instant she saw me.
“Come in, little brother. Today we’ve got corn porridge, white rice porridge with cracklings—fried shallots—plus a salty mung bean pot. Want extra pepper, say so.”
“Corn for me, extra shallot. And a light pot of tea later.”
She worked fast, the ladle CLUNK-CLUNK on the pot wall, corn steam rising sweet and warm. I sat by the counter edge, glanced at the MORNING PORRIDGE board I’d lettered; it had darkened one tone, standing calm like a doorman. Through the third-floor window across the street, the steel bridge cut the view; an aether car slid by, its shadow sweeping a gray stripe along a wall, then vanishing into the bend.

The bottle-picker uncle pushed his rattly cart over, shoulders under a small sign that read SCRAP COLLECTED — SMALL REPAIRS. He saw me and smiled, tight-lipped.
“That sign you wrote for me—people point me right in. I’ve got two bags of caps this morning, and a chipped brass kettle. Mind if I share your stool?”
“Sit, uncle,” the stall owner said. “What’ll you eat? Pho?”
He nodded. “Pho, so I’m full.”
“Give me a second, you two.”
She brought porridge and pho. “All right, two bowls for two people.” He lifted his, blew at the steam, the pho scent lifting with fried shallots and cilantro, and ate with the kind of relief that needs no words. I took my bowl, blew once; sweet corn touched tongue, warmth softening the chest.
He chuckled and lowered his voice, chin tilting toward the lane. “Short news on Lan Tich Lake—cultivators are losing memory. Some forget five minutes. Some ten. Some a few hours.”

“Could be a relic or a beast surfacing.” Plausible enough; certain beasts train forbidden arts around forgetting—lower-stage cultivators get hit.

He lifted his chin. “Two rumors. One: the Realm-Spirit of the secret realm pulled a veil—if you accidentally see something you shouldn’t, it wipes the bit for safety. Wiped clean. No strings attached. Two: the realm spawned a core noetic thing about memory, dreams, or forgetting—the closer you get, the more you forget.”

The owner cut in, flat as tea: “Rumor is rumor, but the Name Shrine hasn’t pasted any heavy warnings. Just says keep off the bank after the auspicious hour.”

The bottle-picker nodded. “Yeah, and when they come out—their eyes shimmer like they just crawled out of a dream.”

I set down my chopsticks. “Dream’s still better than nightmare.”

He said, “Best not to be curious. I pick bottles in the world I live in, not inside that lake.”
I ate two more spoons. The spoon tapped the bowl—tiny as the city nodding to itself.

I paid and went back to the tea stall. A small swell rolled through my head. The Nameglass thrummed like someone had flicked it: Other Sky Channel (Name Shrine) — War Chess — Sword Heaven Branch (Twelve-District Circuit) has opened. Heaven’s Dao supervising; Name Shrine broadcast license in effect; expected delay: two to six watches.

I pocketed the Nameglass. Today’s problem wasn’t how many times to die; it was how to live so the letters still bit.

I lifted the spare plank and drew a frame—new Price Board, tight, small letters. It read:

Small board — 60–120
Medium board — 180–360
Large board — 600–900
Patch & repair — 30–90

Barter option: directions or an administrative stamp.

Bottom-right seam, I stitched a courtesy line: ink by Hua Du — Zhongzhou. That credit stays per yesterday’s deal with Hua Du; his ink went smooth, my letters hardening at the edges as if a hidden mold lived underneath.

“Three degrees tilt, mister,” the candied-haw kid waved his skewer. “Three degrees, life’s three percent lighter.”

“How’d you calculate three percent?” I asked.

“Extrapolated emotion,” he said—dead serious. “Learned it from you.”

Felt like knocking him on the head. Didn’t. I wrote a tiny DON’T on the board’s hem. He burst out laughing and ran.

——

Across the street, Ta Hai leaned against a post, a strip ruler clipped between his fingers, polished bill cap throwing gleam. He tugged his grin wider, pulled out a tin letter mold and SMACKED it onto a sheet: HALF THE PRICE.
Two newbie hands stopped half a beat, glanced at the tin, then at my board—and walked on. The tin sign shouted too bright; the letters were dented.
Ta Hai tossed a line like a stone: “Overdoing letters wrongs the world, eh?”
I didn’t answer. Just tugged the hanging line so the arch sat even.

An old woman with a bundle of incense stopped in front of me. “Write me a mourning board. Date, name, and the phrase ‘clear the way.’”
I checked her Nameglass, opened a ledger line, then set the brush down. Black flowed clean without losing breath.
Ta Hai cut in: “Half the price at my place.”
She looked at the warped tin and shook her head. “Mourning isn’t stamped from molds.” She handed me the board to keep the seal.

He sneered, snapped the mold with a CRACK, then kicked his sign’s footstand a hair farther into the lane, as if measuring people and ground.
I lifted my frame half a step and gave way, silent. In my head I logged a broken-nail icon—the kind I use to remember faces.
He flipped his toolbox shut with a click, the remaining eye flicking toward the tea stall like he was marking it. “Long afternoon.” Two wardens swept lamps down the lane, pausing half a beat at the footstand trespassing into the path. Ta Hai pulled the stand back by a hair, like nothing—lamps slid on.

I returned the board to the old woman with a little slip tucked under the seal. Incense smoke mixed with tea steam; the street flowed again, but that broken nail stayed where it was—for me to pry out later.

A man in a sand-colored long coat stopped by the table. Gold-tier Nameglass glinted on his hand. Clean-shaved, shoes shined enough to mirror aether lines.

“I need a storefront board in thirty minutes,” he said, voice unhurried. “I’ll pay in L-stones.”

I looked at the wood. “Shop name? Lines?”

“TOOLS HONEST,” he said. “And… add: we are not stupid.”

I swallowed a laugh. “Two hundred fifty shardlets.” He nodded. A soft murmur swelled behind me.

I dipped the brush; by the third letter, a man leaned on a pillar with a smile already photograph-perfect. The perfect smile. He cleared his throat twice—and the alley’s eyes drifted. A girl fake-stunned. A boy fake-snorted so exactly timed the sky should’ve blushed.

Points didn’t budge. The stroke did. Emotional slope is real—crowds that happen climb like the heart; staged scenes draw a straight line. I turned my wrist, cut a clean path, let TOOLS breathe one finger joint from HONEST.

The haw-kid peeked from behind the pillar and gulped audibly. I tilted the board so he could study the O-into-N join—tight as a greased manhole lid. He let out a tiny whoa, then showed the locksmith: “Rush locks need to fit this tight too.”
Grown-ups nodded an oh-right nod; ink scent met tea steam, easy on the head.

He looked very close and whispered, “Mr. Hua’s mastic ink… it really makes the letters shine.”
I nodded. “Thanks for running it over.”
His eyes went a little wet—counted as grown-up business. The locksmith joked, “When he gets paid for this board, the ink runner gets a cut!”
The alley went ahhh; the kid laughed too loud, then clapped a hand over his mouth.

I went back to the board. Three lines under TOOLS HONEST:

Line one: fasten true—don’t let nails lie.
Line two: a saw’s smile means it bites the right wood.
Line three: Violet-Lamp Alley — rates straight, letters straight.

[UI: +15 curiosity; +11 approval; +9 glee]

I flipped the underside and laid a small line near the foot—small enough to be polite, clear enough to read:
mastic ink — Hua Du
“This credit line stays,” I said, voice flat. “Per our agreement.”
Gold Coat glanced, then dipped his chin: “So long as it’s not bigger than the shop name.”
“Never,” I said, setting the administrative stamp along the edge and handing the board over.
Hua Du wasn’t here, but the promise in the ink drum and the credit line stood like a handprint.

Gold Coat planted his fists on his hips. “Pretty,” he said. “I’ll handle the administrative seal—doesn’t make the letters prettier, but it helps me sleep. Your pay.”

I passed him the board. He melted into the crowd. The perfect smile dissolved too, as if it never was. Only the ink smell remained.

***

Wind under the bridge turned, carrying machine oil with a thin incense thread from the corner shrine. The city’s sound stepped down one notch—exactly when letters rose on the Nameglass—[Name Shrine News]—voice blank and urgent: A Core-Knotting group is fighting over the Heaven-grade art Hundred Meridians Full Circuit in a secret realm; estimated five casualties at Core-Knotting.

The stranger with the named sword Painted Skin surfaced again near Cloud-Sword Sect; the sect master ordered the gates shut—no one in or out.

The ping cadence tapped the heart’s membrane. One. Then another. I put it away.

——

Purple dusk crawled up the pipes. I looked back over the day. The letters bit. The points… were real—light, but tight.

In the little storeroom behind the tea stall, I locked the door. Called the board with a thought. The Shop Drawer slid out like a medicine chest.

Breathwork (passive) — First-Tier Lv.1 — intake ≈ five percent of a basic Aether Core.
Sturdiness — First-Tier Lv.1 — 1000 points
Quickness — First-Tier Lv.1 — 1000 points
Sword Intent — First-Tier Lv.1 — 1000 points
First-Tier Skill Points — 100 points per 1 Skill Point.

My finger ran along the lip of an empty drawer. Didn’t open it. Not today. Discipline means not climbing just because the numbers tease.

I closed the drawer. Ink still on my hands. Out front, Tham Lang handed me a cup.

I rinsed the brush in the clean bowl and hung it from the bamboo rack. Tham Lang glanced at the Price Board, gave the tiniest nod—like a stamp. She set the pot down. Her eyes tracked the lane. “The bell… will ring.”

I looked. The bell at the mouth of the alley rang twice. Paused. Then three. The auspicious hour, on time.

The Nameglass pried open a hairline ribbon of text: Among the Ten Saint-Venerables of Everlife Forest, Saint-Venerable Thap Nhi Lieu has taken a disciple after five thousand years; her disciple’s name is To Suong.

Three times in a single afternoon. I laid my palm on the counter. The wood passed the alley’s beat into my bones.

“Tomorrow, I’m going into Genesis Stage,” I said, low, packing the tools, pinning the latch. The alley’s breath slowed; the bell rested.

“Mm.” Tham Lang slid a folded quarter-sheet from her pocketbook and pushed it over. “Intro breath drill of Cloud-Sword Sect—External Affairs copy.”

My hand stalled. “How’d you get this?”

“An old debt repaid on paper,” she said. “Not a secret text—just a rhythm check for those with unopened cores. Usable.”

“What’s the price?”

“Write a few more boards tomorrow, and we’re even.”

I lifted a corner of the paper a palm wide: a faint seal at the edge, dry herbal smell. I folded it, slipped it into my jacket; the weight in my chest dropped one rung—at least there was a path.

The haw-kid ran by with a skewer down to the last fruit. “You want it?” he asked.

“I’ll take it,” I said. He rose on his toes and planted the skewer into the Price Board like setting a flag. “For feng shui.”

[UI: +10 → Balance: 850]

I wrapped the brushes, wiped the ink tray.
“Lend me the storeroom for two hours tomorrow,” I said.
“Done. Finish a few boards in the morning, then go in,” Tham Lang nodded.

I jotted a line on the Nameglass: “Tomorrow noon: enter the Noetic Realm (Battle Mesh).”
The storeroom key TICKED in my pocket—like a prelude count-off.

“Sleep early. Tomorrow’s busy,” she said.
“Right. I’ll go in there and get capital,” I said. Before sleep, I tried a 4–4–6–2 breath per the paper. The breath wouldn’t drop into my belly, catching in the chest like a broken stair—there was a core, but it wasn’t open.

The alley bell stopped like the shift had ended. I killed the side lamp and slid the bar. In my head only the breath-stair was left and a bright door—the storeroom door.
The steel bridge trembled one thin beat like a count—city keeping time with me.
