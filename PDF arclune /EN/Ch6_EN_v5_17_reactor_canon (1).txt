Chapter 6 — Genesis, Entering the Noetic Realm

Day 1

12:00 noon. Sunlight as thin as silk on the aether lines. The bell tower breathed even.

I finished the morning’s leftovers—small boards, a couple of price reminders. About one hundred eighty shardlets into the pouch.

The Nameglass thrummed; a line slid across like someone tapping a spoon to a glass rim:
News Brief (Battle Mesh): “Secret realm TS-4 — Lan Tich Lake: seal time extended; rumors of time dilation exceeding 5×. Name Shrine advises keeping order when the bell shifts phase.”

The Nameglass flashed another line:
“A thunder cultivator shattered the renegade of Cloud-Sword Sect’s Seven-Soul Armor; the Saint claims he uses a self-created art called Blood-Soul Thunder Canon. His voice was deep and loud: ‘I, Thunder Heaven Shade, am the bane of all heretics. Heretics meeting me is sons meeting father.’ He laughed big—looks like a free-and-easy Saint.”

Tham Lang met my eyes over the tea counter. “Done with the usual? Don’t forget the other thing.”
She pointed at the back door. “Safe storeroom’s inside. Do it there.”
“I want one more pass around the alley,” I said.

I took a short loop—helped the sanitation uncle pry up a stuck manhole lid, tightened a slack aether wire on a lamp post, pinned a slipped price note for the porridge lady, wedged a counter leg so it wouldn’t creak. No one hired me; I didn’t take pay. People looked, gave small nods, a few sealed-lip laughs. Points fell in a thin drizzle; the faint tick in my head crept upward.

A kid pressed his eye to the door crack. I flicked a test stroke on a scrap of wood. He “whoa’d,” then ran off to show someone.

Noon tapped shallow on the tin roofs. I walked another half-loop to “catch breath.” At the row’s head, the perfect-smile man waited—shirt pressed like a poster. He extended a media “cooperation” card, the corner stamped with the Zhongzhou Merchants Guild logo.

“You said no last time,” he crooned sweet as cane. “But… someone I know in the Guild… wants to help.”
I looked at the card, then at the smile for one beat longer. “If that person truly wants to help,” I said, “let them say it themselves. Don’t borrow the name.”
The porters paused; someone snorted. The card stayed in his hand; I hadn’t touched it. The smile didn’t drop, only thinned by a hair.
“Very well,” he slid the card back, voice sweeter by one degree. “It’s easier to talk in the arena.”
“Up to you,” I said. “Just don’t let the smile make your grip slick.”
He turned. A tiny wrinkle appeared on his perfect back, sharp enough to nick a picky eye. I finished the loop; the count nudged again.

A green flare lit in my head: enough to buy a Skill. Something cold pressed the nape, a coin-touch that sent a thin ring across the scalp. A faint iron taste rose to the tongue, then thinned and went.

15:00. I returned to the tea stall as afternoon tilted. Helped Tham Lang with bowls, sorted cup sleeves. The outer curtain breathed. Outside, “tu…tu” — two short notes from a child’s flute. Tham Lang tilted her head: “Follow me.” I stepped into the storeroom; she closed the door, voice low. “Stand straight. Look here.”
Her fingertip pressed across my breastbone—one point warm, one trace cool. “Calm-Breath Seal. It’s a breath waypoint; it will dissolve by itself in seven nights.”
“Inhale 4-4-6-2. Let the breath drop to the belly; don’t let it get stuck in the chest.”
I followed. By the third count, a coin-sized warmth formed just beneath the sternum; breath fell as if into a well.
She nodded. “You’ve got a core. Not open. That’s enough for the next step.”

I nodded. “Then we do it by the book.” She set a waxed paper sachet on the table, a small smoky vial, and a steaming cup with a bamboo mouthpiece. “Shardlets ground fine. This tincture—two drops only. Don’t swallow rocks.”
I poured shardlets onto the paper, rolled the pestle. Silver dust glided like chalk; the shining grit vanished through the sieve. She tipped the vial; two drops fell—faint mint, a pinch of salt, a ghost of mastic.
“That’s Breath-Guiding Tincture,” she said. “It coats the grains, stops scratches, pulls breath down the correct axis. Keep the 4-4-6-2. Nine cycles is one round. At most three rounds, then rest.”
I loaded the steamer, warmed it until the surface only trembled. The bamboo tube to my lips. The seal below the sternum glowed like a small lamp. *CLUNK*—a steel pipe somewhere tapped the floor from the side passage; oil-scent thinned into the room. Outside, someone tossed a loud bargain: “Half price, yeah?” A small crowd chuckled sharp. I didn’t change cadence.

“Inhale four… hold four…” I counted. “Exhale six… rest two.”
The breath had a taste: mint, salt, something like sun-softened pine resin. A hair-thin itch ran under the skin, ants across the forearm bridge, then gone. I entered the second cycle. Both hands on the navel; heat rose under the palms with the count.
“That’s right,” Tham Lang said, even. “Don’t be greedy. Small dose. If your ears ring, stop.”

Third cycle, my back went light; the navel warmed like a small ember. I held cadence. Fourth. Fifth. On the sixth, the seal beneath the sternum twitched—a tiny *CLUNK*, like a fingernail flicked at the furnace frame. Right at that *CLUNK*, a low flute note sounded in the alley; people formed a triangle wedge, narrowing the main path. Wind struck the corrugated sheet and bent, pressing voices against wooden walls in little waves.

“Continue,” she said. “Do not change cadence.”
I finished nine cycles. Breath slid clean into the belly then rose along the spine—a small loop closed and latched. I set the cup down; sweat pearled only at the temples.
“Second round?” I asked.
She watched my face a beat, reading ink under skin. “Yes. Keep the dose.”
I refreshed the water, added two more drops. The mint thinned; the storeroom wall took two soft knocks like a clock. The mastic in the tincture deepened; the same smell drifted outside the counter—someone had lit “decorative” incense. I settled the mind below the navel, locked my shoulders, and barred the noise from the chest.

The Calm-Breath Seal kept time. Fourth cycle, the ribs stitched speed like a sewing machine. Eighth, warmth pulled from the navel straight through the back, touched the left shoulder blade, then thinned. I exhaled six long counts, rested two.
“Enough for tonight,” she said.
I nodded, but the door felt ajar—if I stopped now, I could still enter tomorrow. “One last round.”
She was silent a beat. “Final round, two and a half drops. Stop when it’s done.”
I did as told.
Ninth cycle, a second light *CLUNK* touched the breastbone—the cage received breath. Behind the heart, a vertebra seated with a small grainy click; the ribs answered with an elastic creak. Heat ran one finger-width up the spine and steadied there. I set the tube down and sat, listening to the body count by itself. Outside, a voice: “Half price and laser-etch?” Laughter swelled. Someone stood with their back to the storeroom door. Tham Lang called through: “There’s a watcher at the back. Your job is to breathe.” She slid in a warm cup of salted tea. “Lock the core.”

I drank, lay on my right, palm on the navel, keeping that place warm. Pulse lowered, deeper. The storeroom wood and tea smell stepped up half a notch.
“Genesis Stage, First Layer,” she said very softly—an official stamp. “Just this for tonight. The seal fades in seven nights. After that, if you want speed, learn a method that increases throughput.”
I nodded. “Favor.”
“Don’t call it a favor. Your pay is tomorrow’s boards,” she said, the corner of her mouth moving. “And—don’t write when your hands still tremble.”
I smiled. “Sleep.” She drew the door half-to, her voice tiny from outside: “This door is open. Live first, then get strong.” I closed my eyes and felt the body: circuits sealed; the frame “clicked” one step tighter. Muscles didn’t swell; they thickened by exactly a thin layer. The shoulder seams pulled a hair like half a size added.

Skin brightened half a tone, more even, visibly alive; veins sat neatly, less risen. The heart beat deep and even, each strike surer; lungs opened a little more room—breath in a bit longer without effort. The hand’s grip increased by a shade; joints ran smooth; steps grew lighter, yet bit the ground better.
Sight sharpened by half a notch; ears caught the tiny rustles that used to slip by. The whole body adjusted “from 1 to 1.5”: no flamboyance, only compact and settled; lifespan felt like it lengthened by a fine line on the calendar—thin but real. Genesis Stage — G1: clean, precise, enough that every movement had an extra half-measure of surety.


Day 2 — 07:00. I opened the cloth purse and kneeled on the tiles behind the counter. Shardlets rolled out, glittering like grains of salt. I spread them into a thin layer.

Hand raised the Nameglass. The pane warmed; a thin scan line passed the stone surface, a small “tick” like a fingernail on glass.

The Nameglass read a clear 783 shardlets. Last night’s grinding deducted—a handful, negligible. I stepped out to the tea stall. “Help me convert seven L-stones into my Nameglass wallet?”

“Can do,” she said. I pushed the pouch with seven hundred shardlets. “Exchange.” Tham Lang nodded, opened her Nameglass, tapped Transfer → @Uyen. A green dot hopped along the screen edge.

[@Tham → @Uyen +7 L-stones — memo: cash exchange COMPLETE]

The wallet in my hand shivered. The Nameglass showed:
Uyen
7 L-stones

She pulled the stone pouch back to herself. “Thanks.” I stowed the Nameglass, tied the empty pouch, and dropped it in the gear box. “I need to enter the Noetic Realm.”

She smiled: “I’ll keep watch.”

“You won’t sell tea?”

“Missing a day is fine.” She pulled down the bamboo shade, hung the CLOSED tag, and barred the counter. “Storeroom. I’ll hold the door.”
I nodded. “If anything’s off?”
“I hit first,” she said. “Go.”
I hugged the Nameglass, stepped into the storeroom, leaned my back to the supply chest, closed my eyes, and called the board—Linebound—by thought. A drawer slid out in my mind:

> Sturdiness — First-Tier Lv.1 — 1000 points.
> Note: strengthens bone-tendon integrity, consolidates muscle, raises blunt-hit tolerance (low at Lv.1); no bulking, no change in silhouette.

I touched it. Cold like biting a coin. In my mind a man stood in a dirt yard. Shoulders broad from carrying, not from posing. Hands callused like bark, finger-joint cracks turned into their own script. At dawn he held the stance until calves shook like a pulled cord; at noon he poured medicine from a black clay pot: eucommia, teasel root, achyranthes, processed rehmannia—dense as kitchen smoke; afternoon he bathed in the brew, skin flushed red, eyes never leaving the water clock; at night he salved, wrapped the hurts, then struck wood, moved stone, hauled rope. Next day repeat. And the next. Steam from the brew pricked the eyes; the yard kept the smell of iron filings and old bark. When he wrung the cloth, the red water rang the bowl like a low bell.

On a seven-night rain, the yard turned to sucking mud; he pressed a stone over his thighs, held breath until lips went purple. On a day so bright his shadow measured half a hand, he still locked the joints, drove the breath, set center as if casting a new frame around the old. Over time, his muscle didn’t swell from excitement; it tightened from discipline. Tendons didn’t rasp from fatigue; they slicked from familiarity. Skin didn’t harden from glues; it thickened from having met enough blows.

Then one very quiet night—quiet enough to hear the moon slide past the eave—he broke something nameless. Not bone. A latch deep in the back sprang: *click*. His breath dropped beneath the navel, then rose, as if pulling a long string back home; blood-energy filled his limbs like fire through copper pipes. Morning after, he stood taller by half a finger, heavier by a bowl of water, steady like a pillar with a new ring clamp. One test punch into a wooden post kicked off a powder mist like dew. That was Genesis G1—no clamor, but clear. He had no core, yet he did it.

The insight held him there awhile, to smell the medicine again, to see the rains and suns printed on the yard, to hear the *click* once more. Then it loosened and withdrew like fog.

Awareness returned to the storeroom. Sturdiness Lv.1 sat in the body—bones pinned, muscle drawn, skin braced—only a very small beginning of that road.

My fists closed by themselves—not to look strong, but to mark that *click* as the target, not a trick.

Warmth flowed along the ligaments. The frame cinched like taking a band; fibers tightened by a half; the outer flesh thickened one impact layer. Not for show. When I gripped the brush and tested a stroke, the wrist held a steadier beat; the tip crossed a wood ridge without wobble. I breathed out. Good—the craft survived. The wrist held true as if two fine wires had been pulled tight under the skin; the brush heel settled without chatter.

I thought of *him*, the man who reached Genesis with sweat, medicine, years.
And me, a broke ghost who had only stepped under the lintel—nothing to compare.

I sat against the wall, heels set. Fingers touched the Nameglass; the login frame appeared, administrative voice: 

>  Connect to the Noetic Realm
> • The Noetic Realm is a hive of intent; it uses participants’ consciousness to raise fidelity.
> • Integrated with the Battle Mesh. On login, the Nameglass locks identity and confirms payment. The Mesh will guide your intent into the Noetic Realm.
> • Intent departs; body stays. Always choose a safe place before connecting.
> • When needed, silently think “log out” three times to leave the Noetic Realm.

The Nameglass blinked the fee: 6 L-stones per session (deducted from the Nameglass wallet, Metropolis rate).
     Confirm / Cancel

I confirmed. Darkness poured like ink; then light returned—not light, but something the mind could read. My ears flexed with a dry little pop, like stepping from tunnel to platform; a tinny after-taste sat at the back of the tongue.

The Noetic Realm.

It felt like waking from sleep. Space silver-dim. The ground like dense fog; maybe not ground—just a plane you could trust. No face, no eyes; I saw by intent. My body was a single-color manikin with no extra details. Everything gave off a very pale glow—just enough to mark edges.

A bass hum lived under the floor-plane, steady as a long wire under current; it pressed the air just enough to touch the teeth.

I was in a small room with four soft walls, a door straight ahead. A small interface hovered forty centimeters in front of me:

> Welcome to the Noetic Realm. You are in the Waiting Room.
> Appearance: Shadow-Figure (intent lacks specific shape; change appearance by imagination).
> Customize by intent; identity locked by Nameglass.
> Modes:
> • Matchmaking: random pairing, up to 15 minutes per match. Foes will not exceed your strength by more than two minor sub-stages.
> • Roam: simulate a random place in the real world.
> • Auction: trade Noetic Shards and special Noetic items.
> • Last-Stand: 100 people in an arena; the last surviving wins. Foes will not exceed your strength by more than three minor sub-stages.
> • Holy War: (locked — insufficient clearance).
> • Free: you will be transferred to the nearest stronghold.
> Note: Interface is visible to you alone. The Realm can simulate your body and “muscle memory” from the real world by scanning intent strength.

[Do you consent to scan intent strength to simulate your body and muscle memory from the real world?]
   Consent / Decline
I consented.

I tightened my fist a shade. Sturdiness Lv.1 had re-set the body—bones pinned, muscle drawn, skin braced—just enough to meet the first pain.

Down at the edge of sight, a ticker crawled slow:
> “Lan Tich Lake (TS-4): first and second teams prioritized left-lane flow; reported memory loss 09:07-09:30 under verification. Name Shrine advises citizens not to crowd near portal gates.”

I inhaled. Intent touched the Free mode. My body flashed once and vanished.
