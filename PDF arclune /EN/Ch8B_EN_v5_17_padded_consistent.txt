Chapter 8B — A Small Debt (Bridge)

Day 2

Night. I opened my eyes. The chest cinched as if I’d just broken the surface, and a long breath ripped out. Limbs tingled—ants carrying each other along the veins. For a blink, the spiraling noetic letters were still washing across my vision, not yet gone. The Battle Mesh had slipped its anchor and returned my intent to flesh.

Wood ceiling. Old tea. A low oil lamp. Outside, damp wind curled through the door gap, dragging street-night and cooled tea into the room. In the opposite building, a late window, later light.

I forced myself up. The spine clicked like someone gently breaking a string of chopsticks. The heart beat again to the normal rhythm of a world with weight.

The wrists remembered the staff, the knuckles the bite of the ring. The nerves still ticked at the edges as if thin ideoglyphs were fading off the bones—ghost-letters lifting like steam. I flexed each finger, naming them one by one in my head, so the hand would come back as a hand and not a tool that forgot its owner.


The bamboo curtain stirred. Tham Lang stepped in, a steaming pot in hand. She set a small pouch on the table and sealed it with a sentence: “Six L-stones in here. Use them to turn the wheel. In the Noetic Realm the fees stack.”

[UI: +1 care]

I touched the pouch. Heavy, like a brick wrapped in cloth. Six was enough not to have to think. Six was enough to owe.

“I’ll borrow four,” I said, voice still hoarse. “The other two—I’ll write boards tomorrow and pay them back.”

She held my eyes. “Sure?”

“Sure.”

She nodded. No smile, no edge—just a nod like closing a ledger page. “Then four. Don’t let next time turn into deeper debt.”

I tightened my hand around the pouch, cold seeping through the cloth. “Owe you.”

She poured tea. “Rest tonight. The body is still borrowing strength.”

I sipped. Heat slid through the chest. My heartbeat gathered itself like a fair crowd letting out after dusk.

“Brother!” A small head slid in through the door. It arrived before I could remember his name. “The last stick, the real last stick!” He waved his candied-haw skewer like a victory flag.

“News from where?” I asked.

“There’s a package. You pick the right bag.” He thrust out a RATE CARD FOR NEWS & CANDY (v2):
— Basic: 2 shardlets (one-line news + a candy).
— Standard: 3 shardlets (two lines + wind direction).
— Premium: 5 shardlets (three lines + one warranty if I blow the whistle off-key).
— VIP: 8 shardlets (foot-path map + drone sweep arc + which heel is worn).
— VVIP: 10 shardlets (everything in VIP + a Shrine blind-spot).

“Shrine blind-spot?” I arched a brow.

“Means a spot the Name Shrine drone slides past without a glance,” he said, solemn. “A blind-spot. Not a tax.”
A blind-spot wasn’t magic; it was math. Drones swept in arcs—three beats over the plaza, a diagonal lick along the bridge ribs, then a lazy turn around the gutter mouths. Stand too proud and the green would taste your eyes; keep half a shoulder behind a column and you were a smudge in somebody else’s story. Kids gave names to math because names were easier to sell.


I swallowed a smile. “Standard today.”

“A-lo-chuant-teh,” he mispronounced on purpose, palm open. “Three shardlets.”

I dropped three into his hand.

He recited, as if memorized: “Outside there’s a fake board, printed letters, ink not fully dry; four men guard it: one smiles but the eyes don’t, one talks sweeter than lard, one carries a steel pipe, one stands half-turned away. And the inker stands off, tapping time. I counted. Oh—and the wind presses toward the tin sheet of the pipe guy. If you pass near that sheet, it throws back machine-oil smell.”

“The drone?”

“At 19:05 it scans a diagonal. Stand tight by the second column—the light sweep won’t hit your eyes. I call it the blink-spot.”

“Whose heels are worn?”

“Left heel on the half-turned man. Right heel on the smiling man. The sweet-talker’s shoes are brand new—the studs still smell like shop.”

“Tip?” I asked.

“Tip is a bowl of congee.” He grinned. “I can eat a small bowl.”

“The congee aunt charges separately,” Tham Lang said without looking—but she set a bowl out anyway.

“Yes, I’ll pay. I have a blind-spot fund.” He nodded like a banker, hugged the bowl, and slurped happiness out loud.

“Does the Name Shrine charge VAT?” I asked him.

“If it does, it charges with its eyes,” he said, grave as an old man.

I passed him a slip. “When you hear the *click*, don’t blow right away. Count to seven, then blow in a low tone.”

“If I blow too high?”

“Then you use the Premium warranty.” I winked.

He laughed, grabbed his whistle, and vanished like smoke with feet.

Outside, the aether engines on the bridge paused for three breaths. The hollow hush. The street held its breath. Right then, the four shifted positions like pieces on a drawn board:
— Ta Hai turned the tin sheet half a notch; the cord scraped.
— Perfect Smile raised the fake board one *li*—a hair’s breadth—his smile neither thinner nor thicker.
— Sweet-Voice opened a “temporary occupancy” notice by half a palm-width, then folded it back, as if letting the crowd’s eyes lick the letters.
— Tran Quat slid his right heel, keeping the left just inside the tile edge—not breaking the rule. The steel pipe like a dash now planted in the side path.
Air thinned. Tin held its breath with us. Somewhere a kettle clicked and failed to boil. The city’s lungs made a small, disciplined pause, the kind a tailor makes before cutting straight through cloth. Perfumes separated—ink here, oil there, salt-sweat and brass—so cleanly that I could almost slice them by scent.


Hua Du did not move. Under a pillar’s shadow, he tapped out the count with his fingers: tap—tap—tap. Even. He looked toward the tea shop like a man staring at a spirit level, waiting for the bubble to center.

The Name Shrine drone skimmed by, green light washing a cold circle. Voices dropped to a whisper. Sometimes a city’s silence feels like complicity. The bell stayed mute.

I slipped the four L-stones under the inner coat. That weight sat against the ribs and cooled into a decision very quickly.

“Boards tomorrow,” I said. “Two medium, one small. Enough to balance two L-stones.”

“Enough is good,” Tham Lang said—neither praise nor blame. “If you’re short, I’ll make up one last time.”

[UI: +1 care]

I looked at her. No joke. “Won’t be short.”

She didn’t argue. She set the pot back in its exact place. The pot belly turned toward the stove like a creature that knew its den. “Rest. Don’t try to act sober the instant you leave the Noetic Realm.”

I nodded. The heart had returned to the city’s normal tempo. A little fog still clung to the edges of the mind.

In the hollow hush, Perfect Smile rehearsed his lines to the air itself.

“Can you write it for half?”—he lifted his voice a notch, pretending to be a hayseed in town.
“Half of what?”—his little henchman asked, shoulders up, eyes scanning like they were searching pockets.
“Half means half!”—Perfect Smile answered, smiling like it wasn’t real.

Sweet-Voice cut in: “Don’t make it look fake. Be reasonably dumb. For example: ‘Write for half, but can you do one-and-a-half quality?’”

They snorted. “Dumb with logic.”

Tran Quat stamped heel to stone—THUMP. “When he talks, press your back, raise a wall. Don’t crowd with the chest—crowd with the shoulder. The shoulder is the body’s lawyer.”

“Nice,” Sweet-Voice nodded. “Shoulder is the lawyer, mouth is the actor, the paper is the prop.”

Ta Hai patted the tin sheet. “Wind is the director.”

“And the bell?” one asked.

“The bell is the censors,” Perfect Smile said mildly. “Our film is folk-grade. Mind the beats. Don’t let the censors… censor.”

They laughed under their breath. The laughter dropped and didn’t shatter.

I let my shoulders go. The hollow in the bones had fully shut. Breathe in, hold three counts, breathe out. Stop counting—counting only brings the Noetic Realm back to mind.

“You plan to step out now?” Tham Lang asked.

“No.” I shook my head. “Let them get tired first.”

“They don’t tire,” she said. “They practice being tired for a living.”

“Then I’ll let them get bored,” I said.

She tilted her mouth. “People out there aren’t easy to bore. They prefer watching to sleeping.”

I stared up at the ceiling and counted the cracks. The shoulders lightened. Thoughts thinned.

The candy-haw kid poked his head in again, carrying the whistle like evidence.

“I’ll test-blow.” He blew long; the pitch climbed too high, almost cracked the surface of the tea.

“That’s F,” I said, stern. “Don’t F.”

“I know,” he winced. “I blew wrong to test the warranty.”

“How does the warranty work?”

“I blow wrong once; next time I blow the mi right for free.” Hands on hips. “Those are the rules.”

“Whose rules?”

“The seller’s,” he grinned, harmless, “but the buyer’s happy too.”

I couldn’t help it; I laughed. I pulled out a thin wood ticket, wrote a few words, and pressed a red seal with my thumbnail: WARRANTY SLIP — NOTE MI. I handed it over.

“Wow,” his eyes went round. “Red seal. Will the Name Shrine recognize it?”

“No. The street will.”

“That’s enough,” he nodded, tucking the slip away like hiding gold.

The hollow hush passed. The aether train keyed on again; the bridge trembled once. The four left their posts without hurry, without drag. Each left a faint odor in the air: machine oil, ink, steel-powder sweat, cheap perfume.

“Tomorrow,” said Perfect Smile, chin lift small enough not to be caught by a lip reader.
“Tomorrow,” said Sweet-Voice, tucking the paper under arm like a dried fish.
“Tomorrow,” Tran Quat lifted the steel pipe out of the side path and set it down as gently as putting a heavy word in the exact place in a sentence.
Ta Hai didn’t speak; he just watched the tin sheet like a chess piece that obeyed.

Hua Du didn’t say “tomorrow.” He only stopped tapping, wiped his fingertips dry on his hem. A number had been marked in his head: two quiet days meant a BIG SIGN. He turned his back one beat before the others—a habit of a man who didn’t want anyone measuring his stride.

Before ink met column, I laid out the day like tiles: two shopfront nameboards, one alley marker. Medium boards needed steady shoulders and a breath you could fold over a whole line without shaking. Small boards wanted speed and a single stroke that refused to apologize.

I opened the ledger and wrote:
— Have: 1 L-stone in the Nameglass, 66 shardlets.
— Borrowed: 4 L-stones from Tham Lang.
— Short: at least 2 L-stones to cover fees.

Route: take Violet-Lamp Alley first while the morning shade still hugs the posts; then the noodle stall at the bridge foot once steam starts to rise. Board specs sat in muscle memory: cedar if I could bully the price down, otherwise pine sealed with cheap resin; two coats for a shopfront, one and a burnish for the alley marker. Don’t argue fonts with men who eat late—just show three strokes clean enough to quiet a table.


I put the brush down; the ink dried fast. In the room, only tea bubbled. Outside, the street was tight as a string not yet plucked. The bell stayed quiet. People kept waiting.
I called Linebound in my head, by thought alone.

I smirked.
Tomorrow, I have to go out. Write boards. And answer whether I can still keep a path through the middle of the people.
